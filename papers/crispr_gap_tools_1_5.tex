\begin{abstract}
Public CRISPR tooling concentrates on a narrow slice of the editing problem.
We attack five evidence-backed gaps with working tools benchmarked on real
public data: (1) cross-dataset collapse of guide efficacy models, which we
quantify on Doench-2016 FC+RES, Doench V1, the DeepHF U2OS screen and the
DeepSpCas9 HEK293T dataset (Kim 2019);
(2) absence of calibrated probabilities from off-target scorers, addressed
with isotonic/Platt calibration on 25,632 crisprSQL measurements;
(3) pegRNA data scarcity, attacked with transfer learning from Cas9 efficacy
data to the 22,956-pegRNA PRIDICT2 dataset; (4) PAM-variant-blind off-target
scoring; (5) chromatin-context-blind off-target scoring. All claims are
bounded by held-out, group-aware evaluation; negative results are reported.
Beyond the five gap tools, the analysis surfaces a \emph{discovery}: a
target-inherent transfer residual -- the per-guide error of a
source-trained model replicates at $\rho=0.86$--$0.90$ across three
independent nuclease screens ($n=53{,}937$ matched guides), more stably
than the biological signal itself ($\rho\approx0.73$). The residual is
predictable from sequence and chromatin features and yields \textbf{PORTA},
a portability correction that rescues cross-dataset transfer from
$\rho=0.080$ to $0.524$ (SpCas9-HF1) and $0.052$ to $0.562$ (eSpCas9)
with zero destination labels, and exposes a per-guide portability score
no current tool reports. On calibrated off-target scoring, five fully-seeded
guide-grouped splits of crisprSQL yield a meta-finding: every model's
AUROC swings by ${\sim}0.2$ across splits and inits (GNN $0.45$--$0.69$),
dwarfing model gaps -- single-split published comparisons on
negative-scarce corpora are irreproducible by construction. The strongest model is a
position-specific logistic regression ($0.692 \pm 0.154$), beating the
GNN ($0.609 \pm 0.084$) on 4/5 identical splits; no deep model
separates from the classical baselines (CFD $0.552$, MIT $0.516$). A natural per-study routing hypothesis (GNN for
low-prevalence GUIDE-seq-style studies, MIT otherwise) is \emph{falsified}
under leave-one-out evaluation: the apparent per-study GNN wins rest on as
few as two positives, and the robust policy on this corpus is the MIT
baseline everywhere (Section~6). All artifacts -- code, 67-test offline suite, data pipelines,
figures, and this paper's numbers -- regenerate from committed scripts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Computational CRISPR tooling has converged on a narrow problem shape:
SpCas9 on-target efficacy in a handful of human cell lines, and off-target
\emph{ranking} scores for NGG-PAM targets. Auditing the public tool and
dataset landscape surfaces five gaps, each backed by dataset-level evidence:
(1)~efficacy models are rarely evaluated across independent assays, and
reported performance collapses when they are; (2)~the widely used MIT and
CFD off-target scores are uncalibrated ranking heuristics -- no published
tool returns a probability of cleavage with an error bound; (3)~prime-editing
pegRNA design models are data-scarce relative to Cas9 nuclease models by two
orders of magnitude (22,956 measured pegRNAs vs.\ millions of Cas9 guide
measurements), yet transfer from Cas9 data is essentially untried in public
tools; (4)~off-target scorers are trained and validated almost exclusively on
NGG-PAM off-targets even though 47\% of measured off-targets in crisprSQL
carry non-NGG PAMs; (5)~chromatin context, known to modulate cleavage
genome-wide, is absent from the standard sequence-only scoring stack despite
crisprSQL shipping CTCF, DNase, RRBS, H3K4me3 and DRIP tracks for every one
of its 25,632 measured pairs. We build one tool per gap and report honest,
group-aware held-out numbers, including where the gap resisted us.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i)~A measurement harness for cross-dataset generalization of efficacy
models, with the first cross-family transfer matrix on Doench/DeepHF
(Section~5). (ii)~A calibrated multi-study off-target benchmark with full
split/init determinism, establishing that negative-scarce corpora cannot
adjudicate between scorers on single splits -- and that per-study
apparent wins, including our own, are positive-count artifacts (a
study-routing hypothesis is stated and falsified, Section~6). (iii)~A controlled study of
Cas9$\to$pegRNA transfer, establishing an honest negative at three
scales in two cell lines (Section~7). (iv)~PAM- and chromatin-aware
off-target channels; five-seed replication reverses our own single-split
finding -- no channel's delta exceeds split noise, and PAM alone
\emph{hurts} on average (Section~8). (v)~A named, quantified, falsifiable discovery --
the target-inherent transfer residual -- and PORTA, the correction it
enables (Section~12). (vi)~A reproducible codebase: 40 hermetic tests,
public data only, every figure regenerated from result files (Appendix~A).

\section{Gap 1: Cross-dataset generalization of efficacy models}
\begin{definition}[Cross-dataset generalization gap]
Let $\mathcal{D}_A, \mathcal{D}_B$ be guide-efficacy datasets with score
distributions $P_A, P_B$, and $f_A$ a model trained on $\mathcal{D}_A$ with
held-out Spearman $\rho_{AA}$ and transfer Spearman $\rho_{AB}$. The
generalization gap is $g(A\!\to\!B) = \rho_{AA} - \rho_{AB}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{proposition}[Identity of indiscernibles for rank transfer]\label{prop:indisc}
If $P_B$ is an affine rescaling of the conditional score law of $P_A$, i.e.\
$Y_B = aY_A + b$ a.s.\ with $a>0$, then $g(A\!\to\!B) = 0$ for any model
$f_A$ that is optimal under squared loss on $\mathcal{D}_A$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Spearman correlation is invariant under strictly increasing transformations
of either argument. If $Y_B = aY_A+b$ with $a>0$ then
$\rho(f_A(X), Y_B) = \rho(f_A(X), aY_A+b) = \rho(f_A(X), Y_A)$, and an
$P_A$-optimal predictor attains the same rank correlation on $B$ as on $A$,
so $\rho_{AB}=\rho_{AA}$ and $g=0$.
\end{proof}
The contrapositive is the empirical content of the gap: observed
$g \gg 0$ certifies that the conditional score laws differ beyond affine
rescaling (different assay, cell type, or delivery), which is what our
harness measures (Section~4.1).

\section{Gap 2: Calibrated cleavage probabilities}
Published scores (MIT/Hsu-2013, CFD/Doench-2016) are ranking scores, not
probabilities. We fit monotone calibrators $c: \mathbb{R}\to[0,1]$ on a
held-out validation slice and evaluate with expected calibration error
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n}\, \big|\,\mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m)\,\big|,
\end{equation}
over equal-width bins $B_m$, and the Brier score
$\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (p_i - y_i)^2$.
\begin{proposition}[Brier decomposition implies ECE lower bound]
For any binned predictor with per-bin confidence $\bar p_m$ and accuracy
$\bar y_m$, $\mathrm{BS} \ge \sum_m \frac{|B_m|}{n}(\bar y_m - \bar p_m)^2$,
hence calibration error contributes quadratically to Brier while ECE
measures it in absolute terms.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The Murphy decomposition gives
$\mathrm{BS} = \sum_m \frac{|B_m|}{n}(\bar y_m - \bar p_m)^2
+ \sum_m \frac{|B_m|}{n}\,\mathrm{Var}(p \mid B_m)
- \mathrm{Var}(Y)$ up to the standard reliability--resolution--uncertainty
terms; the reliability term is exactly the sum of squared per-bin
calibration gaps, and variance terms are nonnegative.
\end{proof}

\section{Gap 3: Transfer learning for pegRNA efficiency}
% fully-convolutional trunk; why length-agnostic weight transfer is valid
The conv trunk applies the same filters $\{W_k\}$ to any input length $L$:
$h^{(L)} = \phi(W * x^{(L)})$, and global average pooling
$\bar h = \frac{1}{L}\sum_t h_t$ makes the head length-agnostic. Pretraining
on 30-mer Cas9 contexts and fine-tuning on 99-mer pegRNA contexts therefore
transfers motif detectors without architectural surgery.

\section{Gaps 4 and 5: PAM-variant and chromatin-aware off-target scoring}
We extend a message-passing duplex GNN with a global channel
$g_i = [\,\mathrm{onehot}(\mathrm{PAM}_i);\ \mathbf{e}_i\,]$ concatenated
after pooling, where $\mathbf{e}_i\in\mathbb{R}^5$ is the crisprSQL
epigenetic track vector. The ablation
$\{\varnothing, \mathrm{PAM}, \mathbf{e}, \mathrm{PAM}{+}\mathbf{e}\}$
isolates each channel's contribution on an identical guide-grouped split.
For gap 4 the decisive slice is the non-NGG held-out subset: if PAM
awareness only memorized majority-PAM statistics, non-NGG AUROC would be
unchanged; an increase localizes the feature's value to PAM-variant
contexts, where SpG/SpRY-class editors operate.
\begin{proposition}[Pooling preserves mismatch interaction signals]
Let $G$ be the duplex graph with backbone edges and all-pairs mismatch
edges, and $h_v^{(T)}$ the node states after $T$ message-passing steps with
a GRU update. Then for any two positions $u,v$ carrying mismatches, $h_u$
and $h_v$ are functions of each other's input features for $T\ge 1$, so
mean/max-pooled readout retains mismatch--mismatch interaction information
that position-independent scoring rules (MIT, CFD) discard by construction.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Mismatch nodes are mutually adjacent by construction. One message-passing
step aggregates neighbor messages at every node, so $h_v^{(1)}$ depends on
$x_u$ for every mismatch neighbor $u$; induction extends this to $T>1$.
Mean and max pooling are permutation-invariant functions of
$\{h_v^{(T)}\}$, hence retain these joint features. MIT scores factor as
$\prod_i (1-w_i)$ over mismatch positions and CFD as a product of
per-position terms, so neither can represent any interaction term.
\end{proof}

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Datasets}
All datasets are public and pulled by \texttt{scripts/fetch\_data.sh} or
vendored mirrors; no live calls occur inside the test suite.
\textbf{Doench FC+RES} (5,310 30-mer guide contexts; Azimuth mirror of
Doench et al.\ 2016) with \texttt{score\_drug\_gene\_rank} efficacy in
$[0,1]$. \textbf{Doench V1} (2,144 mouse guides; 34-mer extended contexts
trimmed to the Azimuth 30-mer convention) with Percent Rank efficacy.
\textbf{DeepHF} (55,604 guides with indel frequencies for WT-SpCas9 in
U2OS; Wang et al.\ 2019) as the divergent assay family. \textbf{crisprSQL}
100720 release (25,632 experimentally measured guide/off-target pairs from
17 studies, with cleavage frequencies, five epigenetic tracks,
thermodynamic energies, cell line and study provenance; St\"ortz \&
Minary 2021). An edlib full-alignment audit finds 8{,}949 of the 25{,}632
pairs (34.9\%) carry at least one insertion or deletion
(\texttt{results/gap2\_edlib\_baseline.json}) -- substitution-only
scorers (CFD, MIT/Hsu) structurally cannot score a third of the corpus. The 25{,}632 pairs measure only 87 unique guides,
13 of them (14.9\%) by two or more studies
(Fig.~\ref{fig:guide_upset}) -- the guide-level dependence this creates
is why all gap-2 evaluations use guide-clustered splits and SEs. A networkx guide--guide graph (edge = two guides measured against a
shared locus) is nearly empty: 83 guides, 23 edges, density $0.0068$,
largest connected component 10, and 65 guides (78\%) isolated
(\texttt{results/offtarget\_network.json}). The empirical off-target
evidence base is therefore a constellation of single-guide studies,
not an interconnected corpus -- no cross-guide statistical borrowing
is possible.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{fig_guide_upset.pdf}
\caption{UpSet plot of guide-sharing across crisprSQL studies. Most
guides are measured by exactly one study; a core set (e.g.\ the VEGFA

Bare edit distance itself is a weak scorer (grouped-split AUROC $0.51$,
3 seeds), confirming that indel-aware energy terms, not indel counting,
are what matter. \textbf{PRIDICT2 23k} (22,956 pegRNAs with 99-mer wide
target contexts, engineered PBS/RT features and measured editing
efficiencies in HEK293T and K562; Koeppel et al.\ 2023, underlying screens
from Mathis et al.\ 2023).
\subsection{Models and protocols}
Efficacy models: ridge on one-hot+dinucleotide+GC features, and a
fully-convolutional CNN (two Conv1d layers, global mean+max pooling).
Off-target models: a duplex message-passing GNN (base-pair identity,
mismatch flag and position node features; backbone plus all-pairs mismatch
edges; GRU updates) with optional global PAM/epigenetic channels.
Baselines: MIT/Hsu-2013 and CFD/Doench-2016 reimplemented from the primary
sources (CFD via the published CRISPOR reference matrices). Every split is
group-aware (by guide for off-target, by pegRNA group for gap 3) so no
near-duplicate leaks between train and test; calibration is fit only on a
validation slice; test sets keep natural class prevalence so ECE is
meaningful. Hyperparameters are fixed before evaluation; seeds are
reported with every number.
\subsection{Reproduction}
\texttt{python -m crisprgap.train\_efficacy},
\texttt{python -m crisprgap.train\_offtarget},
\texttt{python -m crisprgap.train\_offtarget\_ablation},
\texttt{python -m crisprgap.train\_pegrna}; harnesses in
\texttt{crisprgap/crossdata.py} and \texttt{scripts/run\_crossdata\_cnn.py}.
The hermetic suite (\texttt{pytest}) runs 40 tests offline.

\subsection{Gap 1: collapse across divergent assays, transfer within families}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{figs/fig_transfer_matrix.png}
\caption{Gap 1 transfer matrix (ridge, seed 0): within-family transfer is
moderate ($0.43$--$0.50$) but every cross-family entry collapses to
$\le 0.13$, against within-screen $0.73$. The assay-family boundary, not
model capacity, is the binding constraint.}
\label{fig:matrix}
\end{figure}
Ridge models trained on Doench FC+RES transfer to Doench V1 at
$\rho=0.501$ (within: $0.455$) and V1$\to$FC+RES at $0.434$ (within:
$0.458$): inside the Doench assay family the score laws are near
affine-equivalent and Proposition~\ref{prop:indisc} applies. Across assay families
transfer drops to a weak positive residue: FC+RES$\to$DeepHF
$\rho=0.138$ and V1$\to$DeepHF $0.123$, while DeepHF is highly
learnable within itself ($0.732$) -- a generalization gap of ${\sim}0.6$. The
bottleneck is therefore not model capacity or feature quality but assay
distribution shift -- the quantified form of the gap (Figure~\ref{fig:crossdata},
Table~\ref{tab:crossdata}). A CNN under a tight compute budget shows the
same structure (Table~\ref{tab:crosscnn}): positive but weak transfer
to DeepHF ($0.16$ from FC+RES, $0.04$ from V1) against within-dataset
$0.64$.
Within-dataset CNN numbers are undertrained at this budget and are reported
as such.
\begin{figure}[h]\centering\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{fig_crossdata.pdf}
\caption{Within-dataset vs cross-dataset Spearman (ridge, 3 seeds).}
\label{fig:crossdata}\end{figure}
Adding a third assay family -- DeepSpCas9 (12,832 HEK293T contexts, Kim
et al.\ 2019) -- to the CNN matrix (Table~\ref{tab:crosscnn}) sharpens
the picture: DeepSpCas9 is highly learnable within itself
($\rho=0.498$), transfers \emph{positively} to and from the Doench
family ($0.18$--$0.26$), yet its exchange with DeepHF remains weakly negative in both
directions ($-0.08$ and $-0.09$). The earlier stronger anti-correlation
($-0.12$/$-0.22$) was partly an artifact of the DeepHF sequence-encoding
bug below. Extending the matrix to six datasets (adding CRISPRon TRAP12K
and sgDesigner, both HEK293T-family) resolves the structure: the three
HEK293T-assay datasets transfer positively among themselves
($0.19$--$0.46$) and with the Doench family ($0.02$--$0.36$), while
DeepHF \emph{as a source} transfers weakly negative to all three
HEK293T-cluster datasets ($-0.03$ to $-0.08$); transfer \emph{into}
DeepHF is mixed ($-0.09$ from DeepSpCas9, $-0.06$ from sgDesigner,
$+0.10$ from CRISPRon). The surviving boundary is a DeepHF-source
effect: a model fit on U2OS indel frequencies ranks guides
anti-consistently with HEK293T-family readouts. Two datasets
also exhibit cross-exceeds-within inversions (CRISPRon within $0.06$ but
$0.27$ to DeepSpCas9; sgDesigner within $0.33$ but $0.46$ from
DeepSpCas9): CRISPRon's Day-2 labels are noise-limited and 57\% of
sgDesigner scores are tied at the 100\% ceiling, so their own labels
bound their within-dataset numbers below what transfer can recover.
\paragraph{A shared-library ceiling on residual methods.} The residual
concordance that powers the discovery (Section~\ref{sec:discovery})
requires the same guides assayed under two conditions. That structure is
rare: across the three HEK293T-cluster datasets, guide-core overlap is
zero (DeepSpCas9 $\cap$ sgDesigner: 0 of 12{,}825/1{,}309 cores;
sgDesigner $\cap$ CRISPRon: 0; DeepSpCas9 $\cap$ CRISPRon: 54). DeepHF's
three-nuclease-over-one-library design remains the only corpus in this
work where residual concordance is computable -- a structural limit on
how far the method, and cross-dataset residual claims generally, can be
extended without new co-assayed libraries.
\paragraph{A seventh family: CRISPRscan (zebrafish in vivo).} Adding the
CRISPRscan in-vivo screen (Moreno-Mateos et al.\ 2015, Nat Methods;
492 guides with measured indel activity, Nature ESM MOESM633) as a
seventh dataset -- the only non-mammalian, non-cell-culture assay in the
matrix -- the boundary sharpens further (ridge, 3 seeds,
\texttt{results/crisprscan\_transfer.json}). CRISPRscan is learnable
within itself ($0.33$--$0.40$), but almost nothing transfers across the
species/assay line: Doench-family models are negative into zebrafish
($-0.02$ to $-0.11$), CRISPRon and sgDesigner are zero, and DeepHF is
\emph{strongly anti-predictive in both directions} ($-0.29$ to $-0.32$)
-- the DeepHF-source anomaly therefore is not HEK-specific but reflects
a deeper U2OS-screen peculiarity. The single positive bridge is
DeepSpCas9 ($+0.08$ into zebrafish, $+0.13$--$+0.15$ out), plausibly
because its synthetic 30-mer contexts force sequence-only learning. The
gap-1 verdict hardens: cross-dataset generalization fails by assay family
\emph{and} by species, and the failure magnitude tracks biological
distance. The CNN row/column (2 seeds;
\texttt{results/crossdata\_cnn\_7ds\_crisprscan\_seed*.json})
replicates the bridge structure: DeepSpCas9$\to$CRISPRscan stays positive
($+0.19$/$+0.18$) and DeepHF stays negative in both directions
($-0.06$/$-0.14$ out, $-0.26$/$-0.27$ in), while CRISPRscan's own CNN
within-score collapses to $0.11$--$0.15$ (vs ridge $0.33$--$0.40$) --
492 guides are too few for the CNN, a clean small-data regime example. A
seed-0 appearance of CRISPRscan$\to$sgDesigner $+0.32$ did not replicate
on seed 1 ($-0.07$): exactly the single-seed artifact class this paper
warns about, preserved here as such.

\paragraph{Eleven datasets: two transfer communities and one inversion.}
The DeepCRISPR regression release (Chuai et al.\ 2018, Genome Biol;
bm2-lab/DeepCRISPR \texttt{paper\_data-regression}) contributes four
further guide-level screens -- HCT116 (4,239) and HeLa (8,101), derived
from Wang et al.\ 2014; HL60 (2,076), derived from Xu et al.\ 2015; and a
DeepCRISPR-compiled HEK293T set (4,566) -- taking the ridge matrix to
11${\times}$11 (3 seeds, identical harness;
\texttt{results/crossdata\_ridge\_11ds.json}). The enlarged matrix
resolves the transfer graph into \emph{two communities}. The
Wang/Xu-derived screens predict each other well (HCT116$\to$HeLa
$+0.34$, HeLa$\to$HCT116 $+0.37$, HL60$\leftrightarrow$both
$+0.26$--$+0.30$) but barely transfer into the HEK-family datasets
(${\le}0.10$ in either direction), while the HEK-family core
(fcres/v1/DeepSpCas9/sgDesigner, $+0.41$--$+0.56$ mutual) ignores the
Wang/Xu community in return (${\le}0.17$). Dataset provenance -- who
ran the screen, not which cell line or which label -- is the dominant
predictor of transferability, and the two communities are separated by
exactly one editorial boundary (Wang 2014 / Xu 2015 vs the
HEK-centered studies). A rapidfuzz leakage audit of unique 20-mers
confirms the community structure is not a leakage artifact
(\texttt{results/leakage\_audit.json}): crisprscan, sgDesigner,
sgRNAScorer and the Wang/Xu side share \emph{zero} identical or
edit-distance-1 guides with each other or with the HEK family. The one
exception is fcres$\leftrightarrow$v1: 1{,}830 shared 20-mers (fcres
re-screens the v1 library by design), so the fcres$\to$v1 transfer score
($+0.50$) is the one matrix entry that guide overlap inflates; it is not
load-bearing for any conclusion. The anomaly is the DeepCRISPR-compiled HEK293T
set: it is weakly learnable within (ridge $0.161$) and
\emph{anti-correlated with nine of the ten other datasets} (out
$-0.24$--$-0.06$; in $-0.28$--$-0.06$), including its own Wang-family
siblings (HCT116$\to$dc\_hek293t $-0.13$, HeLa$\to$ $-0.12$); the sole
exception is the DeepHF pair, which is flat rather than negative
($+0.03$ out, $0.00$ in). A
label-inversion or systematic sign artifact in that released table is
the most parsimonious reading; we keep it in the matrix as a stress
test and flag it rather than discard it, since every other dataset's
row and column is sign-consistent with its within-score. CRISPRon, the
remaining HEK-family member, sits oddly between communities (positive
into the Wang/Xu set, $+0.17$--$+0.31$, near-zero out of it) --
unresolved. The community reading is formalized with Louvain community
detection (networkx) on the symmetrized positive-part transfer graph:
the partition is identical across three seeds (modularity $Q=0.295$),
placing the four HEK-family datasets plus dc\_hek293t in one community
and the Wang/Xu screens, CRISPRon and CRISPRscan in the other
(\texttt{results/transfer\_communities.json}).

\paragraph{A twelfth family: the DepMap consensus label.} DepMap 23Q4
Public (figshare article 24667905) provides Chronos-inferred per-guide
efficacy for 193,475 guides pooled across $>$1,000 screened cell models
-- the largest efficacy label in existence, and a consensus across
lines rather than a single assay. It is learnable within (ridge
$0.328$, 3 seeds; \texttt{results/crossdata\_ridge\_12ds.json}) but
transfers only weakly in both directions ($\le 0.12$ into it; its best
out-edge is HL60 $+0.24$). Re-running Louvain on the $12\times 12$
matrix leaves the two-community structure intact (identical partition
across 3 seeds, $Q=0.300$; \texttt{results/transfer\_communities\_12ds.json}):
DepMap binds to no community strongly and lands in the Wang/Xu side on
its weak positive edges. An independent Leiden partition (leidenalg
0.12.0 / python-igraph 1.0.0, 3 seeds;
\texttt{results/transfer\_communities\_leiden.json}) recovers the
identical two-community assignment, so the structure is not an artifact
of the Louvain heuristic.

\paragraph{Pooling closes the gap only inside a community.} Ridge
trained on pooled HEK-community data (fcres+v1+DeepSpCas9+sgDesigner,
8k guides each) transfers to held-out DeepHF at $0.08$--$0.11$; a pooled
Wang/Xu-side training set (HCT116+HL60+CRISPRon) reaches held-out HeLa
at $0.31$--$0.33$. The swapped pools score ${\le}0.02$ and ${\le}0.01$
respectively (3 seeds;
\texttt{results/pooled\_community\_transfer.json}). More data closes the
gap only when it comes from the same community -- the barrier is not
sample size. A biopython feature audit (GC, nearest-neighbor RNA--DNA
$T_m$, length; added to ridge on fcres and HeLa) changes within- and
cross-scores by ${\le}0.01$ (3 seeds;
\texttt{results/biopython\_feature\_audit.json}): standard biophysical
features neither improve within-dataset fit nor rescue cross-community
transfer.

\paragraph{External binary test: sgRNAScorer 2.0.} The Chari lab's
sgRNAScorer 2.0 ships its training split as 215 high- and 215
low-activity guides (rajchari2/sgrnascorer; the model itself reads only
the first 20 nt of each listed sequence). We use the split untouched as
an external discrimination endpoint
(\texttt{results/sgrnascorer\_transfer.json}). A ridge trained on
Doench-2016 fcres \emph{anti-predicts} it (AUROC $0.464$), as does v1
($0.439$); CRISPRscan ($0.635$) and sgDesigner ($0.656$) transfer
positively but far below the within-Chari 5-fold ridge ($0.807$). The
two communities behave differently even on a binary endpoint, and the
HEK-family training data is not merely weak here but actively
misleading. The position-wise logos
(Fig.~\ref{fig:chari_logo}) show the split concentrates at
PAM-proximal positions (largest single-position frequency shift 0.42
at position 20; \texttt{results/chari\_logo.json}).

\subsection{Data-integrity audit: the DeepHF encoding bug}
\label{sec:encoding-audit}
A mid-project audit of the DeepHF pkl arrays caught a decoding error:
the arrays are (n, 22) integer matrices whose first column is a fixed
sentinel (value 1 on every row) and whose remaining 21 columns encode
bases as $2{=}C, 3{=}G, 4{=}T, 5{=}A$ -- verified by correlating the
decoded GC content against the bundled biofeature GC column
($|\rho|=0.966$). Our initial loader read 5 as N, which zeroed
${\sim}25\%$ of one-hot positions and prepended a spurious base.
Every DeepHF-derived result in this paper (PORTA, the residual
discovery, the cross-dataset matrices, the pooling analysis) has been
regenerated from the corrected loader; the discovery
($\rho=0.845$--$0.900$ residual concordance) and PORTA's corrected
transfer ($0.524$/$0.562$) are essentially unchanged, while the
CNN cross-family transfer numbers move from spuriously negative
($-0.25$) to weakly positive ($+0.04$ to $+0.16$). The artifact
direction is instructive: corrupting $25\%$ of positions did not
merely add noise -- it flipped the sign of cross-family transfer for
the CNN (but not ridge), a failure mode that single-dataset validation
cannot detect.
\subsection{Gap 1 (cont.): pooling does not fix the boundary}
Naive multi-dataset pooling fails both ways: Doench-pooled training still
transfers to DeepHF at only $\rho=0.118$, and adding Doench data to DeepHF
training slightly \emph{hurts} held-out DeepHF ($0.67$--$0.69$ pooled vs.\
$0.72$--$0.74$ pure, 3 seeds). The assay-family boundary, not dataset size,
is the binding constraint (Table~\ref{tab:pooled}).

We rebuilt this benchmark for full determinism (seeded torch init after
catching unseeded-run variance) and ran five fully-seeded guide-grouped
splits (held-out pair counts 487--1,079, natural prevalence).
Table~\ref{tab:offtarget} gives the matrix: the GNN averages
$0.609 \pm 0.084$ against CFD $0.552 \pm 0.094$ and MIT
$0.516 \pm 0.119$, leading on mean but winning only 3/5 and 2/5 of
splits respectively, with every model's AUROC ranging ${\sim}0.2$ across
splits and inits (GNN $0.454$--$0.685$). On the canonical seed-0 split,
the paired bootstrap interval for the GNN-minus-MIT difference includes
zero ($-0.043$ [$-0.115$, $+0.025$]). \textbf{Meta-finding: on
negative-scarce multi-study corpora, split and init variance dwarfs
model gaps -- single-split published comparisons on such corpora are
irreproducible by construction.} Calibration is equally unstable:
isotonic recalibration on each split's small validation slice raises mean
ECE ($0.326 \to 0.389$, helping only 1 of 5 splits), reversing the
textbook expectation at this sample size (Table~\ref{tab:calib}).
\paragraph{A one-hot logistic beats the GNN.}
The canonical strong-but-simple baseline for this task -- logistic
regression on one-hot guide$\times$off-target base pairings at each
position -- reaches $0.692 \pm 0.154$ on the identical five splits,
beating the GNN on 4/5 (paired mean $+0.083$), CFD on 4/5 and MIT on
4/5, with per-seed AUROC spanning $0.446$--$0.930$. The strongest model
on crisprSQL is therefore not the GNN but a linear model over positional
mismatch indicators, and the entire model ordering is small beside the
split-to-split swing ($0.484$ within the logistic model alone). This
strengthens the meta-finding: on negative-scarce corpora, model choice is
second-order to evaluation design.
\paragraph{Inferential layer: per-position mismatch effects.} Refitting a
reduced main-effects model (one mismatch indicator per position, 20
features) on the seed-0 train split with guide-cluster-robust standard
errors (statsmodels GLM, Binomial;
\texttt{results/poslog\_inference.json}) gives an interpretable effect
profile -- and it does \emph{not} reproduce the textbook seed gradient.
The strongest protective effects sit at positions 1 ($\hat\beta=-0.65$,
95\% CI $[-1.17, -0.13]$), 12 ($-0.57$ [$-0.96, -0.18$]) and 11
($-0.48$ [$-0.91, -0.04]$), while the PAM-proximal positions 18--20 are
null and position 15 is significantly \emph{positive}
($+1.21$ [$0.58, 1.83$], cluster-robust $p=1.3\times10^{-4}$). The
position-15 anomaly survives clustering, so it is not a within-guide
correlation artifact; the remaining candidates are base-identity aliasing
(the marginal indicator mixes all 12 substitution types) and study
composition. We report it as an unresolved anomaly rather than a claim.
Clustering matters: naive Wald intervals are 2--3$\times$ narrower, and
three nominally significant positions (4, 17) lose significance under
guide-cluster-robust errors -- a concrete instance of the corpus's
guide-level dependence structure corrupting naive inference.

\paragraph{External validation on the CRISPOR benchmark (Haeussler
2016).} As a held-out-corpus check we evaluated the crisprSQL-trained
positional logistic model on the Haeussler et al.\ 2016 benchmark
(26{,}052 guide--site pairs; 2{,}550 present in crisprSQL and excluded,
leaving 23{,}502 external pairs; \texttt{results/haeussler\_external.json}).
The benchmark carries only 8 validated positives, so all AUROCs are
anecdote-grade: the published scores rank those 8 sites near-perfectly
(MIT $0.998$, bundled CFD $0.985$) while the crisprSQL-trained poslog
inverts ($0.461$). We read this as two cautions, not a verdict: (i) a
model trained at 94\% prevalence does not transfer its ranking to a
0.03\%-prevalence corpus, and (ii) eight positives cannot arbitrate
between scorers. External off-target validation needs corpora with
hundreds of validated negatives \emph{and} positives -- reinforcing the
gap-2 meta-finding. A mechanism probe rules out the obvious fix:
retraining with balanced class weights or random undersampling
(imbalanced-learn 0.14.2) \emph{deepens} the inversion ($0.461 \to
0.385$ and $0.338$; \texttt{results/prevalence\_rebalance.json}), so
prevalence handling is not what separates the published scores here.

\paragraph{Alignment score as a classical comparator.} A
Smith--Waterman local-alignment score between guide and off-target
(parasail, \texttt{dnafull}, match $+2$, gap open/extend $2/1$;
\texttt{results/parasail\_alignment.json}) reaches global AUROC
$0.704$ on the full corpus, but on the guide-grouped held-out slices it
averages $0.557$ across three seeds ($0.630/0.484/0.558$) -- between
CFD ($0.552$) and the positional logistic model ($0.609$) on the same
splits. Overall sequence similarity alone is therefore not enough; the
positional mismatch pattern carries the discriminative signal, which is
what the poslog effect profile makes explicit. A published physics-based
model, CRISPRoff (Alkan et al.\ 2018; vendored from RTH-tools with
positional weights, PAM correction and DNA-opening terms; guide
self-folding term omitted as guide-constant within grouped evaluation),
scores $0.602$ mean AUROC on the same three slices
($0.675/0.529/0.603$; \texttt{results/crisproff\_baseline.json}) --
matching the positional logistic model ($0.609$) and beating MIT, CFD
and the alignment score. 69 pairs (0.3\%) are outside the published
model's internal-loop table (loop $>$11) and were excluded. The
strongest baselines (CRISPRoff, poslog) share exactly the positional
mismatch-energetics structure, and both remain far below the corpus's
imputed ceiling -- the calibrated-probability gap is open.
\paragraph{In-vitro contrast: CFD works much better on CHANGE-seq.}
CHANGE-seq (Lazzarotto 2020, Nat Biotechnol 10.1038/s41587-020-0555-7
MOESM3; absent from the crisprSQL snapshot) measures cleavage of
202{,}043 candidate sites for 110 guides on \emph{cell-free} genomic
DNA. Scoring the 190{,}704 substitution-only sites (5.2\% carry indels
and are unscorable by CFD) with our vendored CFD implementation gives
AUROC $0.741$ for $\geq 20$ reads and $0.799$ for $\geq 100$ reads,
with Spearman $0.514$ between CFD and log-reads across positives
(\texttt{results/changeseq\_cfd.json}). Because the table lists only
detected sites, these AUROCs separate strong from weak cleavage, not
cleaved from uncleaved. The contrast is the point: the same CFD model
that scores $0.552$ on cellular assays scores $0.74$--$0.80$ on the
biochemical readout -- the cellular layer (delivery, chromatin, repair,
nuclease exposure time), not sequence energetics, is what collapses
off-target predictability in cells. The matched rhAmpSeq
validation arm (Table 7, 151 sites with cellular RNP indel rates)
quantifies the drop directly: in-vitro read counts rank-order cellular
indel rates at Spearman $0.577$, and only 17\% of in-vitro-detected
sites reach $>0.1\%$ cellular indel
(\texttt{results/changeseq\_cellular.json}).
\paragraph{Bulge-carrying off-targets in cells (TAPE-seq).} TAPE-seq
(Kim 2022, Nat Commun 10.1038/s41467-022-35743-y, Suppl.\ Data 3;
prime-editor off-targets, cell-based, absent from crisprSQL) types each
site's bulge explicitly. Across 74 off-targets of seven targets, 19\%
carry an RNA or DNA bulge (7 each; \texttt{results/tapeseq\_bulges.json})
-- the same order as the 34.9\% indel-carrying share in crisprSQL's
cellular studies -- and mismatch counts run 1--4. Read-depth differences
between bulge and non-bulge sites are not resolvable at $n=74$ (MWU
$p=0.67$); the honest content is the prevalence number: roughly a fifth
to a third of cellular off-targets sit in the substitution-only scorers'
blind spot.
\paragraph{Mismatch-count decay in a new cellular corpus (Extru-seq).}
Extru-seq's targeted amplicon validation (Kim lab 2022, Genome Biology
10.1186/s13059-022-02842-4, Additional File 5; 309 candidate sites
across seven human and mouse targets, 19-nt spacers, absent from
crisprSQL) gives an independent cellular decay curve
(\texttt{results/extruseq\_validation.json}): validation rates fall from
89\% (one mismatch) to 82\% (two), 48\% (three), 26\% (four) and 0\% at
seven or more; mismatch count correlates with indel rate at
Spearman $\rho=-0.586$. The seed-versus-distal contrast does not
separate here (MWU $p=0.87$, but only 15 seed-only sites), so the
classical seed hierarchy is not independently confirmed at this sample
size. Because the spacers are 19-nt, the 20-mer CFD matrix does not
apply; we report the count-based curve rather than forcing an
approximation.
\paragraph{CFD in cells at therapeutic-guide scale (SURRO-seq).}
SURRO-seq (Liu et al.\ 2022, Nat Commun 10.1038/s41467-022-31543-6,
MOESM6/8; cell-based surrogate assay, absent from crisprSQL) measures
7,250 candidate off-targets across 110 therapeutic guides with indel
frequencies in cells (\texttt{results/surroseq.json}). CFD reaches
AUROC 0.692 against the authors' significance calls and 0.76 at the
IF${}\ge1\%$ level, but only 0.538 at IF${}\ge0.1\%$: the sequence
score orders strong off-targets well and dissolves near the detection
floor, where cellular noise dominates. Its companion systematic
mismatch library (284 single-mismatch sites over five spacers) shows a
positional activity gradient that correlates with the CFD positional
penalties at Spearman 0.893 -- the matrix shape is right in cells --
while per-site agreement is much weaker ($\rho=0.388$), quantifying how
much of single-site behavior is not sequence-position alone.
\paragraph{Cross-cell-type replication (DISCOVER-seq+).} DISCOVER-seq+
(Nat Methods 2023, 10.1038/s41592-023-01840-z, MOESM6/11; in-vivo
break capture, absent from crisprSQL) profiles the same VEGFA-site-2
guide in K562 and iPSC cells
(\texttt{results/discoverseqplus\_replication.json}). The two treatment
arms within a cell type return identical site lists (Jaccard 1.0), but
across cell types the sets overlap at Jaccard 0.289, and replication
falls steeply with mismatch count: 53\% of 3-mismatch K562 sites
recur in iPSC, 47\% at four, 19\% at five, 7\% at six. High-mismatch
off-targets are largely cell-context-specific -- a direct caveat for
any off-target catalogue built in one cell type and applied in
another.
\paragraph{PE-based versus nuclease-based detection (PEAC-seq).}
PEAC-seq (Nat Commun 2022, 10.1038/s41467-022-35086-8, MOESM4--8)
profiles the classic GUIDE-seq panel (VEGFA TS1--3, EMX1, RNF2) with a
prime-editor tag instead of a nuclease
(\texttt{results/peacseq\_crossmethod.json}). Cross-method replication
against crisprSQL's GUIDE-seq sets for the same guides is partial:
Jaccard 0.41 (TS1), 0.28 (TS2), 0.013 (TS3, where PEAC-seq reports 284
sites to GUIDE-seq's 16), 0.125 (EMX1). Sites that both methods detect
carry significantly higher PEAC-seq enrichment (MWU $p=3\times10^{-6}$
TS1, $4\times10^{-10}$ TS2): cross-method agreement is an enrichment
gradient, not a set boundary. Detection chemistry -- not only cell
context -- redraws the off-target map.
\paragraph{Editor chemistry and context (Tracking-seq).} Tracking-seq
(Nat Biotechnol 2024, 10.1038/s41587-024-02307-y, MOESM4--6) applies
one capture chemistry across editors and contexts
(\texttt{results/trackingseq\_heterogeneity.json}). For the same
VEGFA-site-2 guide, the CBE off-target set shares \emph{zero} sites
with Cas9's 239, and PE2/PE3 share 2.5\%; the editor -- not only the
guide -- defines the off-target set. For one Pcsk9 guide across five
mouse contexts, cultured lines overlap at Jaccard 0.10--0.36 while
ex-vivo liver shares \emph{no} site with any cultured context; within
NIH3T3, the Tracking-seq score predicts cross-context replication
(top-quartile sites replicate at 76\% vs 23\%, Spearman 0.54,
$p=3\times10^{-14}$). Off-target catalogues are editor- and
context-specific; scores rank transferability.
Detect-seq (CBE chemistry; Lei 2021, Nat Methods
10.1038/s41592-021-01172-w, MOESM5) puts an independent method on the
same axis (\texttt{results/detectseq\_overlap.json}): against crisprSQL
GUIDE-seq for the same guides (coordinate-verified hg19, sites at 1--3
bp exist), overlap is 5/512 for VEGFA site 2 (Jaccard 0.007), 0/49 for
EMX1, 2/246 for HEK293 site 4 -- the CBE off-target map is essentially
disjoint from the nuclease map, replicating Tracking-seq's zero
CBE--Cas9 overlap on a second platform. Same method, two cell lines
(HEK293 site 4 in HEK293T vs MCF7) replicates far better (Jaccard
0.603). RUNX1 profiled with a Cpf1 base editor yields 950 sites versus
27 for BE4max -- enzyme scaffold changes the off-target burden by an
order of magnitude (PAM-density caveat noted).
EndoV-seq's in-vitro complement (Wienert 2019, Nat Commun
10.1038/s41467-018-07988-z, MOESM4 Supp.\ Fig.\ 1; 102 mismatch
substrates x 3 guides, chromatin-free) gives the biochemical floor
(\texttt{results/endovseq\_tolerance.json}): ABE7.10 retains 66\% of
on-target deamination at a single mismatch (Cas9 cleavage 78\% on the
same substrates), falling to 18\% at two and 8\% at three mismatches;
single mismatches in the PAM-distal region (positions 1--7) retain
86\% of ABE activity versus 42\% in the seed (13--20). The high
in-vitro tolerance against low cellular off-target counts (Detect-seq,
Tracking-seq) localizes base-editor specificity gains to the cellular
context, not the enzyme's intrinsic recognition.
Akcakaya 2018 (Nature 10.1038/s41586-018-0500-9, MOESM4/6; 3,382 +
167 CIRCLE-seq sites, WT and KI conditions) maps the in-vitro capture
landscape (\texttt{results/akcakaya\_circleseq.json}): unlike cellular
assays, the site spectrum is dominated by high-mismatch loci (2,386 of
3,382 at 4 mismatches, only 6 at $\le$1), with median readcount falling
to 9\% of on-target at 2 mismatches and 1\% at 4; WT-versus-KI
readcount concordance is $\rho=00.78$ and $0.65$ for the two targets.
In-vitro capture is far more promiscuous than cellular detection, so
CIRCLE-seq-derived candidate lists need modality-specific calibration
before they can inform cellular off-target probabilities.
BreakTag (Nat Protoc 2025, 10.1038/s41596-025-01271-4, MOESM2/4) adds a
third in-vitro modality (\texttt{results/breaktag.json}): for VEGFA
site 1 it captures 772 candidate sites (hg38) where crisprSQL GUIDE-seq
recorded 36; lifting GUIDE-seq hg19$\to$hg38, 21 of the 36 (58\%) are
rediscovered, but overall Jaccard is 0.027 -- sensitivity has grown
twenty-fold across a decade of assays. BreakTag also resolves scission
geometry by variant: 51\% of SpCas9-WT cuts are blunt-dominant versus
44\% for LZ3 and 47\% for xCas9 (n=1{,}606/347/427), a
variant-dependent cleavage-mode axis that off-target scorers ignore.
A 2025 highly parallel Cas12a variant screen (Nat Commun
10.1038/s41467-025-57150-9, MOESM5; 4,800 targets x 11 PAM contexts x
23 variants) quantifies PAM flexibility directly
(\texttt{results/cas12a\_variants\_pam.json}): mean indel activity is
0.46 at canonical TTTV, 0.29/0.23/0.22 at CTTV/TCTV/TGTV, and
$<0.08$ at TGCV/TTAV/TTCV/TACV; 13 variants with a measurable TTTV
reference retain $>$10\% relative activity at 6--9 alternative PAMs
(engineered LbRVRR and enAsCas12a-HF1 the broadest). PAM-variant
flexibility is a continuous, variant-specific spectrum rather than a
binary PAM rule -- gap-4 models must score PAM context as a graded
variable per variant.
Cas12a-derived base editors show the same graded structure within the
protospacer (Nat Commun 2025, 10.1038/s41467-025-59653-x, MOESM6
Fig.\ 2F; 1,000 cytosine-position measurements):
\texttt{results/cas12abe\_window.json} -- BEACON2's editing window
spans protospacer positions c8--c13 at $>$5\% mean activity with a
c10 peak of 23\%, while positions c1--c7 stay below 5\%; position, not
a binary window label, carries the bystander risk.
\paragraph{What fidelity engineering buys (gap 4).} A 2023 variant
screen (Nat Commun 10.1038/s41467-023-41393-5, MOESM8/9) measures
amplicon indels for WT and high-fidelity SpCas9 variants on the same
GUIDE-seq-detected off-targets
(\texttt{results/hifi\_variants\_offtarget.json}). WT leaves 68\% of its
off-target amplicons active ($\ge0.1\%$ indel); SpCas9-HF1,
eSpCas9-plus, evoSpCas9 and B-HypaR-SpCas9 cut median off-target
activity to 0.2--0.5\% of WT and eliminate 59--79\% of off-targets
outright, and GUIDE-seq off-target counts drop from 10--85 (WT) to
0--2 per site. The cost is on-target: mean on-target indel falls to
71\% (evoSpCas9), 52\% (B-HypaR) and 10\% (B-HeFSpCas9) of WT. The
fidelity--activity tradeoff is continuous and variant-specific, which
is why a single shared penalty table (gap 4) cannot describe variant
off-target behavior.
\paragraph{Variant ladder on on-target activity (Kim screen).} The Kim
et al.\ high-fidelity variant screen (6,481 guides $\times$ WT, Sniper,
eSpCas9(1.1), SpCas9-HF1, HypaCas9, evoCas9; reprocessed deposit in
MOESM10 of 10.1038/s41467-023-41393-5) orders the variants by activity
retention Sniper 1.01 $>$ eSpCas9 0.88 $>$ HF1 0.74 $>$ Hypa 0.64 $>$
evoCas9 0.33 (\texttt{results/kim\_hf\_variant\_on.json}), and HF1's
0.74 mean retention matches DeepHF's 0.70 median on an independent
library -- variant behavior is replicable across datasets even when
guide-level ranks are not. WT-trained ridge transfers to variants at
$0.33$--$0.53$ against within-variant CV $0.49$--$0.81$, the same
transfer gap measured twice more above. The same deposit's mismatch screen
(1,802 guide--target pairs, \texttt{results/kim\_mms\_tolerance.json})
orders single-mismatch tolerance in the opposite direction: median
retention of WT activity at 1-mismatch off-targets falls Sniper 0.84
$>$ eSpCas9 0.27 $>$ HF1 0.14 $>$ Hypa 0.06 $>$ evoCas9 0.006 -- the
fidelity ladder is monotone in mismatch intolerance, and evoCas9's
extreme specificity matches its 33\% activity retention.
\paragraph{Base editors: same tradeoff (evolved ABEs).} The evolved-ABE
screen (Nat Commun 2024, 10.1038/s41467-024-52483-3, MOESM8 S~Fig.~12;
20 guide blocks, 30 OT measurements)
(\texttt{results/be\_evolved\_offtarget.json}) shows the same axis:
ABE8e keeps the highest on-target activity (mean 70\%) but the worst
off-target retention (median 9.5\% of on-target, 53\% of OTs
eliminated), while evolved variants (E2/E4/M1/M3/E18) cut median OT
retention to 0.4--2.3\% with 67--73\% eliminated at 16--38\% mean
on-target activity. Across Cas9 nucleases and base editors alike,
specificity is bought with activity -- no free-lunch variant appears in
any of the four variant corpora analyzed here.

\paragraph{Init-only versus split variance.}
Retraining the GNN on the \emph{identical} seed-0 split with five torch
init draws (data order fixed) gives AUROC $0.530$--$0.606$ (sd $0.032$)
and uncalibrated ECE $0.209$--$0.389$: initialization alone contributes
over a third of the total split-plus-init spread (sd $0.032$ of $0.084$)
and swings calibration error by $0.18$ on byte-identical data. ECE
comparisons between calibration methods on single inits are therefore
uninterpretable at this scale.

Per-study analysis on the seed-0 split (Table~\ref{tab:perstudy})
initially suggested structure -- a GNN win on Anderson-era GUIDE-seq data
($0.764$ vs MIT $0.550$) and a loss on Kim-style targeted libraries
($0.487$ vs $0.692$). \textbf{This structure does not survive audit.}
The Anderson row rests on \emph{two} held-out positives. Across the five
seeded splits, only 13 (study, split) pairs carry at least 30 test pairs
with $\ge$5 positives and $\ge$5 negatives; the GNN wins exactly one of
them. A leave-one-out logistic router on study features (size, prevalence,
mismatch load, PAM mix, GC) collapses to always-MIT (routed AUROC
$0.6283$ $=$ always-MIT $0.6283$; bootstrap 95\% CI for the difference
$[0,0]$), an explicit ``GNN when prevalence $<0.10$'' rule does
\emph{worse} than always-MIT ($0.609$), and even an oracle router gains
only $0.001$ -- there is no routing headroom on this corpus. We therefore
report the study-routing direction as falsified: per-study model rankings
on crisprSQL are not estimable at current positive counts (1,494 validated
negatives across 17 studies), which is itself the corpus's core
limitation. Table~\ref{tab:routing} lists every audited row.

On the PRIDICT2 23k library (grouped held-out pegRNAs, HEK293T), a CNN over
the 99-mer target context beats the engineered-feature ridge baseline
($\rho=0.794$ vs.\ $0.741$). Transferring the Doench-pretrained conv trunk
does \emph{not} beat training from scratch at this scale ($0.789$ vs.\
$0.794$).
\subsubsection{Scarce-regime scaling: a clean negative}
Subsampling pegRNA training data to the truly scarce regime does not rescue
the transfer hypothesis: at $n=250$ scratch reaches $\rho=0.725$ vs.\
transfer $0.724$; at $n=1000$, $0.762$ vs.\ $0.758$; at $n=4000$, $0.771$
vs.\ $0.767$. Cas9 guide-efficacy motifs do not transfer to pegRNA
efficiency at any measured scale. The interpretation is mechanistic: pegRNA
efficiency is dominated by PBS/RT-template geometry and edit context (the
numeric channel alone already gives $0.741$), not by the protospacer motifs
that Cas9-efficacy pretraining encodes. Two findings survive the negative:
(i)~sequence-context CNNs are strong pegRNA predictors even with 250
training examples ($\rho=0.72$), and (ii)~the field's assumption that
prime-editing models should borrow from nuclease models needs evidence, not
analogy. The K562 track replicates the pattern (ridge $0.554$, scratch
$0.610$, transfer $0.588$). A second negative: folding all 22{,}956
mutated target contexts with ViennaRNA RNAfold (40-nt edit-centered
window) and adding the MFE feature to the numeric ridge changes grouped
held-out Spearman from $0.7329$ to $0.7331$. Two further thermodynamic
channels confirm the null: seqfold minimum-ensemble $\Delta G$ on the same
40-nt edit-centered window and primer3 nearest-neighbor protospacer $T_m$
move grouped held-out Spearman by $-6\times10^{-5}$ (HEK) and
$-1\times10^{-4}$ (K562) across three grouped splits
($n=22{,}956$). Three independent thermodynamic/structural tools, zero
signal -- local target secondary structure, at least at this resolution,
is not the missing signal. A final control reframes the gap-3 story: a
LightGBM gradient-boosted model on the \emph{19 engineered numeric
features alone} matches the sequence CNN (HEK $0.795$ vs $0.794$; K562
$0.610$ vs $0.610$, three grouped splits each). The wide-target sequence
channel therefore adds nothing beyond what the engineered edit-geometry
features already encode; SHAP attribution ranks Correction\_Replacement,
Correction\_Length and the RT/PBS melting-temperature features highest,
consistent with the mechanistic reading that edit geometry, not
protospacer motif, drives pegRNA efficiency. Tuning the GBM (30 Optuna
TPE trials on the seed-0 train/val split only) pushes it \emph{past} the
CNN: HEK $0.798$ vs $0.794$, K562 $0.616$ vs $0.610$ (three grouped
splits, frozen config). The strongest model we benchmark for pegRNA
efficacy is therefore a tuned tabular model over engineered edit-geometry
features -- the sequence CNN's advantage is an artifact of comparing
against an untuned baseline. The conclusion is library-consistent: XGBoost
in the same tuned hyperparameter region reproduces it (HEK $0.799$, K562
$0.606$), while \emph{default} XGBoost collapses (HEK $0.742$, K562
$0.476$) -- a 0.06--0.13 Spearman swing from tuning alone, larger than any
architecture difference we measured on this task. A third gradient-boosting
library, CatBoost 1.2.10, completes the check on identical splits
(\texttt{results/pegrna\_catboost.json}): tuned-region CatBoost is the
strongest single method on HEK ($0.8014$, vs tuned LightGBM $0.7978$ and
tuned XGBoost $0.7985$) and ties the CNN-level on K562 ($0.6121$ vs
LightGBM $0.6161$), while CatBoost's \emph{defaults} are the strongest
untuned configuration we tested (HEK $0.7945$). Across all three libraries
every tuned GBM meets or exceeds the sequence CNN on both cell types, and
the ranking LightGBM $\approx$ CatBoost $>$ XGBoost is preserved on both
targets; the GBM advantage is therefore a property of the feature
representation, not of any one library or tuning run. It is also robust to
the \emph{search algorithm}: an independent 30-trial hyperopt TPE search
over the same space on the same seed-0 train/val split converges to a
different configuration (lr $0.067$, 535 trees, 8 leaves) with
indistinguishable held-out performance (HEK $0.7979$ vs Optuna $0.7978$;
K562 $0.6109$ vs $0.6161$; \texttt{results/pegrna\_hyperopt.json}). A
Gaussian-process Bayesian search (scikit-optimize 0.10.2, same budget and
split) converges to yet a third configuration (lr $0.053$, 530 trees, 8
leaves) with the same held-out performance (HEK $0.7986$, K562 $0.6154$;
\texttt{results/pegrna\_skopt.json}): the tuned-GBM conclusion is robust
across three search algorithms from two surrogate families (TPE, GP).

\paragraph{Cross-library transfer works here.} The PRIDICT v1 training
library (Mathis et al.\ 2023, Nat Biotechnol; Nature ESM
\texttt{MOESM5}, Library\_1 sheet: 92{,}423 pegRNAs, HEK293T) gives a
second, independently measured pegRNA corpus. On the minimal shared
feature set (PBS/RT lengths, edit position, correction length,
one-hot correction type), ridge transfers \emph{symmetrically} and
near-losslessly: v1$\to$v2 $0.622$ vs v2-within $0.631$, and
v2$\to$v1 $0.338$ vs v1-within $0.368$ (3 seeds;
\texttt{results/pridict\_v1\_transfer.json}). Cross-library transfer
is therefore attainable when the measurement pipeline is shared --
sharpening the gap-1 negative: the efficacy-transfer failure there is
about assay provenance, not a universal impossibility. We preserve all
negatives as the gap-3 result.
\paragraph{Third external pegRNA library (DeepPE).} Kim 2021's DeepPE
Library 1 (Nat Biotechnol, 10.1038/s41587-020-0677-y MOESM4; 43{,}150
pegRNAs, HEK293T PE2) provides an independent replication of the gap-3
findings (\texttt{results/gap3\_deeppe.json}). A ridge over the same
20 engineered features reaches 5-fold CV Spearman $0.669$ -- below the
PRIDICT2 HEK ridge ($0.74$) but far above chance, replicating the
edit-geometry dominance: PBS GC content ($\rho=+0.35$) and PBS $T_m$
($+0.29$) are the top positive univariate effects, all five MFE
features are negative ($-0.15$ to $-0.30$), matching PRIDICT2's effect
signs across the board. Notably the DeepSpCas9 score \emph{alone}
correlates $0.496$ with pegRNA efficiency -- the Cas9 prior feature
carries real signal in someone else's assay, even though (as shown
above) transferring the Cas9 \emph{sequence model} does not beat
scratch. The two Cas9-transfer ideas are different: a Cas9 activity
\emph{feature} helps; Cas9 motif \emph{weights} do not.
\paragraph{Edit position and multiplicity (DeepPE Library 2).} The
companion position/type library (6{,}152 pegRNAs; 5{,}431 with a
resolvable edit) localizes the edit by lowercase-marked bases in the
3$'$ extension (\texttt{results/gap3\_deeppe\_lib2.json}). Edit
position within the RT template has a weak, non-monotone effect
(Spearman $-0.15$; mean efficiency $8$--$19\%$ across positions
5--19, peaking at position 10), while edit multiplicity is the sharp
filter: 1--2 edited bases average $11.4$--$15.4\%$ but 10-base edits
collapse to $1.9\%$. Substitution-vs-indel identity is not resolved
by this encoding and we do not claim it.

\paragraph{External PAM-variant validation (Koike-Yusa 2014).} The
nbt.2800 Supplementary Table 1 deposits MiSeq cleavage for 190 candidate
off-targets of a single guide (Piga l20\_5tm, mouse ESC), half with
NAG-variant PAMs -- an independent gap-4 test outside crisprSQL
(\texttt{results/koike\_yusa\_nag.json}). The PAM effect is anything but
subtle here: NGG sites with 2--3 mismatches cleave strongly (mean 4.8\%
at 2 mismatches, 11.6\% at 3), while NAG sites stay near background at
every mismatch count ($\le 0.08\%$ mean; one of 95 above 1\%). The 20
stable-subclone replicates and transient transfections agree ($r=0.982$).
CFD ranks these sites with Spearman $-0.08$ overall -- no useful
calibration in the near-background mass -- while still separating the
few high-cleavage sites (AUROC $0.90$ at the $\ge 1\%$ level, but only
3--4 positives). Single guide, single gene, mouse cells: a directional
check that PAM discrimination is strong when measurable, not a broad
benchmark. A statsmodels logistic regression
(cleaved $>0.1\%$ $\sim$ mismatches $+$ NAG) points the right way but
under-resolves at $n=190$ with 15 positives: NAG odds ratio $0.34$
(95\% CI $0.10$--$1.11$, $p=0.074$), per-mismatch OR $0.50$
($0.22$--$1.14$, $p=0.097$) (\texttt{results/koike\_yusa\_stats.json}).
We report the direction and the interval, not a claim.
\paragraph{PAM-variant activity atlas (Kim 2020).} The
DeepSpCas9variants PAM-determination supplement (Kim 2020, Nat
Biotechnol, 10.1038/s41587-020-0537-9 MOESM3) measures
background-subtracted indel frequencies for 13 Cas9 variants across
8{,}130 guide$\times$PAM library members (6-mer library PAMs, first base
fixed to A; we classify on the dinucleotide at positions 2--3). Two
findings matter for gap 4 (\texttt{results/gap4\_pam\_variants.json},
Fig.~\ref{fig:pam_variant}). First, variant rankings do not transfer
from WT: Spearman correlations of per-guide activity with wild-type
SpCas9 drop from $0.83$ (Sniper-Cas9) and $0.66$--$0.74$ (high-fidelity
variants eSpCas9(1.1), SpCas9-HF1, HypaCas9) to $0.29$--$0.60$ for
PAM-expansion variants (VQR, VRQR, VRER, QQR1, VRQR-HF1) -- a
WT-trained scorer cannot rank guides for the engineered enzymes.
Second, the PAM-dinucleotide atlas (Fig.~\ref{fig:pam_variant})
quantifies each variant's relaxed specificity: SpCas9-NG shows activity
across all NG$x$ classes (mean $36.3\%$ at NGG vs $\geq 20\%$ at
NGA/NGC/NGT), xCas9 extends to NA$*$/NT$*$ at reduced levels, and VQR
peaks at NGA ($\sim$20\%) as designed -- while WT SpCas9 falls to
${<}1\%$ outside NG$*$. Off-target models for variant enzymes
therefore need variant-specific PAM channels; a shared PAM penalty
table is structurally wrong.
\paragraph{Cross-enzyme transfer (gap 1 $\times$ gap 4).} Training a
mono+dinucleotide ridge on WT-SpCas9 activities and testing on each
variant's activities over the \emph{same} guides (Kim 2020 MOESM4,
$n=23{,}999$; MOESM5, $n=7{,}999$;
\texttt{results/gap1\_variant\_transfer.json}) transfers poorly:
WT$\to$variant Spearman $0.22$--$0.45$ on MOESM4 and $0.39$--$0.53$ on
MOESM5 (WT$\to$WT within-library holdout: $0.28$ / $0.53$). Sequence
features alone do not carry across enzyme variants even with matched
guides -- variant identity is a distribution shift of its own, and the
DeepSpCas9variants authors' variant-specific fine-tuning is not
optional.
\paragraph{High-fidelity variants (DeepHF, 53,937 matched guides).}
DeepHF (Wang D 2019, Nat Commun 10.1038/s41467-019-12281-8, MOESM3)
measures WT-SpCas9, eSpCas9(1.1) and SpCas9-HF1 efficiency on the same
53,937 guides (\texttt{results/deephf\_transfer.json}). A
position-specific mono+dinucleotide ridge reaches within-enzyme CV
$0.73$--$0.76$ for all three enzymes, but cross-enzyme transfer lands at
$0.56$--$0.66$ -- the same $0.1$--$0.2$ transfer gap as the
PAM-variant library (Kim 2020) -- while the enzymes agree pairwise at
$0.73$--$0.82$. SpCas9-HF1 retains a median 70\% of WT activity, with
only 24\% of guides keeping $\ge$80\% and 4.6\% collapsing below 20\%:
high-fidelity engineering is a real, guide-dependent efficacy cost that
single-enzyme models do not predict.
\paragraph{Cross-condition stability within one screen (CRISPRon).}
The TRAP12K surrogate screen (Xiang 2021, Nat Commun
10.1038/s41467-021-23576-0, MOESM4; 11,446 gRNAs with matched indel
efficiencies at day 2 and days 8/10 $\pm$ dox induction; an on-target
efficacy record, not in crisprSQL) isolates the time/induction axis of
gap 1 (\texttt{results/crispron\_conditions.json}). Day-2 rankings
correlate with later conditions at only Spearman $0.18$--$0.25$, while
day-8 and day-10 ($-$dox) agree at $0.886$; top-decile membership is
shared at 18--65\%. A one-hot ridge trained on day 2 transfers to day
10 at $0.531$ versus $0.638$ within-condition CV (gap $0.107$).
Early-timepoint efficacy measurements are dominated by delivery and
editing kinetics rather than intrinsic guide quality -- screens
harvested early are, in effect, a different assay, which sharpens the
gap-1 rule that assay conditions define the dataset boundary.
\paragraph{Prime-editing design features replicate at 92k scale, cell
context dominates (gap 3).} PRIDICT2.0 Library 1 (Mathis 2023, Nat
Biotechnol 10.1038/s41587-022-01613-7, MOESM5; 92,423 pegRNAs) yields
ridge CV Spearman 0.523 from 19 design-only features
(\texttt{results/pridict2\_pechromatin.json}); a first pass over all
numeric columns scored a perfect 1.0 because per-replicate outcome
columns leak the target -- recorded here as a methods caution. The 2024
chromatin-context screen (Nat Biotechnol
10.1038/s41587-024-02268-2, MOESM3; 16,055 pegRNAs with matched HEK293T,
K562, K562-MLH1dn and adenovirus-delivered measurements) shows
cross-context rank agreement of 0.68--0.96 and a median 2.24-fold
efficiency gain under MLH1 depletion: pegRNA ranking is portable across
cellular contexts far better than Cas9 efficacy rankings (gap 1), and
MMR status is a first-order covariate no sequence-only pegRNA model
captures.
Cross-library transfer on five matched design features (PBS/RT length,
PBS/RT GC, Cas9 score; z-scored per library;
\texttt{results/pe\_cross\_library\_transfer.json}) gives
DeepPE$\to$PRIDICT2 0.194 and PRIDICT2$\to$DeepPE 0.389 against
within-library CV 0.669/0.523: pegRNA design models do not transfer
across library generations even on shared features, the gap-1 boundary
reappearing inside prime editing.
\paragraph{Mismatch tolerance by variant.} The same supplement's
mismatch sheets (3{,}120 guide--target pairs, Day 4 and Day 7;
\texttt{results/gap4\_mismatch\_tolerance.json}) quantify
high-fidelity engineering directly: at one mismatch WT SpCas9 still
cleaves at $31.2\%$ mean (80\% of its on-target $39.1\%$), while
eSpCas9(1.1) $9.0\%$, SpCas9-HF1 $6.5\%$, HypaCas9 $5.2\%$ and evoCas9
$2.0\%$ -- a 3--16$\times$ reduction. Sniper-Cas9 is the exception at
$25.1\%$, barely below WT despite its high-fidelity label. At two
mismatches WT drops to $8.2\%$ and all fidelity variants sit at
${\lesssim}2\%$. Single-mismatch tolerance, not deep-mismatch
tolerance, is where variant choice matters most.

\paragraph{Direct chromatin-track queries: a coverage gap and a power
ceiling.} crisprSQL's bundled chromatin columns are themselves sparse:
on the 1{,}271 K562 hg19 rows (CIRCLE-seq + Cho) the bundled H3K4me3
column is zero for 97.6\% of rows. Querying the ENCODE Broad K562
H3K4me3 signal track directly (UCSC API,
\texttt{wgEncodeBroadHistoneK562H3k4me3StdSig}) at a seeded 400-row
subsample (373 returned) recovers signal everywhere: active off-targets
sit in higher signal (median $1.62$ vs $1.14$), and the track alone
scores AUROC $0.629$ -- but with only 13 negatives the difference is
underpowered (Mann--Whitney $p=0.12$), and the track correlates with the
bundled column at just $\rho=0.26$ where the latter is nonzero.
Direct-track chromatin context therefore exists and points the expected
direction, but crisprSQL's negative scarcity bounds what any chromatin
channel can prove on this corpus -- the honest gap-5 verdict is a power
statement, not an effect statement.
\paragraph{Multi-track control: the bottleneck is negatives, not track
choice.} To test whether the weak single-track result was a track-choice
artifact, we re-ran the direct-query protocol on the identical seed-0
400-locus subsample with three further ENCODE K562 tracks -- H3K27ac
(active enhancer, \texttt{wgEncodeBroadHistoneK562H3k27acStdSig}),
H3K9me3 (heterochromatin, \texttt{wgEncodeBroadHistoneK562H3k9me3StdSig})
and DNase (open chromatin, \texttt{wgEncodeOpenChromDnaseK562SigV2}) --
plus H3K4me3 for within-run consistency
(\texttt{results/gap5\_encode\_multitrack.json}). Per-track AUROCs:
H3K4me3 $0.575$, H3K27ac $0.588$, H3K9me3 $0.439$, DNase $0.508$; no
track reaches significance (Mann--Whitney $p=0.28$--$0.92$) because every
track sees the same 13 negatives. The directional pattern is consistent
with chromatin biology -- active marks trend higher at active off-targets,
the repressive mark trends lower -- but the effect sizes (medians within
$1.1\times$) are far below what 13 negatives can resolve. Track choice is
not the gap-5 bottleneck; the crisprSQL negative count is, and no
additional ENCODE track on this corpus can change that.
\paragraph{A well-powered null on SITE-Seq, and why it cannot settle the
question.} The one crisprSQL cell line with enough negatives for a
powered test is the Cameron SITE-Seq corpus (1{,}630 HEK293T hg38 loci,
893 positive / 737 non-cleaved under the corpus's cleavage$>0$
convention). Querying ENCODE HEK293T DNase-seq (ENCSR000EJR, file
ENCFF529BOG, GRCh38) at all 1{,}630 loci gives a clean null: AUROC
$0.507$, Mann--Whitney $p=0.64$, medians identical to two decimals
($0.0605$ vs $0.0607$). But SITE-Seq measures cleavage on \emph{naked
genomic DNA in vitro} -- chromatin is absent by assay design, so a
chromatin null is expected by construction and says nothing about the
cellular question. The cellular assays in crisprSQL (GUIDE-seq, HTGTS)
contribute $\leq 10$ measured negatives combined across cell types. Gap 5
is therefore \emph{structurally unanswerable} on the largest curated
corpus: the well-powered slices lack chromatin by design, and the
chromatin-relevant slices lack negatives. Any published
chromatin-improves-off-target claim resting on this corpus alone should be
read with that structural caveat. We verified the pattern in a second cell line:
ENCODE K562 DNase-seq (ENCSR000EKN, file ENCFF526LYS, hg19) at all 1{,}299
crisprSQL K562 loci gives AUROC $0.44$ on the 35 negatives
(Mann--Whitney $p=0.24$) and a Spearman of only $0.075$ ($p=0.008$)
between DNase signal and cleavage frequency across the 1{,}236 positives
(\texttt{results/gap5\_k562\_dnase.json}) -- and 1{,}155 of those rows are
CIRCLE-seq, again cell-free by assay design. Peak-call overlap is no better: loci inside a
called DNase peak (ENCFF795GKD narrowPeak) cleave at 97.8\% vs 97.2\%
outside (Fisher odds ratio $1.25$, $p=1.0$;
\texttt{results/gap5\_k562\_peaks.json}). A LOESS trend across the 1{,}236 positives is
flat to slightly negative (Fig.~\ref{fig:k562_loess}; endpoint slope
$-0.27$ log-cleavage per signal unit;
\texttt{results/gap5\_loess.json}). Genic context is likewise flat on the hg38
slice: against GENCODE v47 basic, exonic loci cleave at 53.8\% vs
54.5\% for non-exonic (Fisher OR $0.96$, $p=0.86$;
\texttt{results/gap5\_genic\_context.json}). A bioframe re-implementation of the overlap
(independent interval engine, 0-based conversion) reproduces the null:
exonic 53.5\% vs 54.9\%, OR $0.94$, $p=0.79$
(\texttt{results/genic\_bioframe\_xcheck.json}). Two further K562 tracks -- CTCF (ENCFF014YLT) and
H3K27ac (ENCFF010PHG) fold-change bigWigs -- show the same: AUROC
$0.42$ / $0.49$ on the negatives ($p=0.11$ / $0.79$) and positive-side
Spearman $0.084$ / $0.043$ (\texttt{results/gap5\_k562\_multitrack.json}).
Three chromatin modalities, two cell lines, seven readouts: no signal.
A systematic ENCODE per-modality sweep widens the test to eight further
accessions: K562 H3K4me3 (ENCFF291SWG), H3K4me1 (ENCFF526QTS),
H3K36me3 (ENCFF745HXR), H3K27me3 (ENCFF312LYO) and H3K9me3
(ENCFF834YLI) ChIP fold-change tracks, K562 ATAC-seq (ENCFF915UDE) and
an independent K562 DNase-seq p-value track (ENCFF643IZX) at the 1{,}271
K562 loci, plus HEK293T ATAC-seq (ENCFF128DKF) at the 1{,}629 hg38 loci
(\texttt{results/gap5\_encode\_sweep.json}). Every AUROC sits between
$0.40$ and $0.52$; the largest positive-side correlations are
${\rho}=0.080$ (ATAC) and $0.077$ (DNase p-value), nominally
$p=0.005$--$0.007$ but neither survives Bonferroni across the eleven
chromatin tracks tested ($\alpha=0.0045$). Extending the sweep to
the HeLa and HAP1 slices (four HeLa-S3 tracks: DNase ENCFF426GCA, ATAC
ENCFF051PGV, H3K27ac ENCFF388WMD, CTCF ENCFF799KLZ at 388 loci; two
HAP1 tracks: DNase ENCFF095QLZ, ATAC ENCFF559JGD at 160 loci;
\texttt{results/gap5\_encode\_sweep2.json}) finds the same null:
AUROCs $0.48$--$0.54$ against few negatives ($n=58$ per line), and
although the largest positive-side correlations of the study appear
here (HeLa ATAC $\rho=0.144$, CTCF $\rho=0.140$, DNase $\rho=0.130$;
nominal $p=0.009$--$0.018$), none survives Bonferroni across the
seventeen chromatin tracks tested ($\alpha=0.0029$). The U2OS slice --
the largest in crisprSQL at 5{,}920 hg19 loci -- has \emph{no} ENCODE
chromatin track of any assay on any assembly, so the chromatin question
is untestable there; we record this as a data-coverage gap. A final batch widens the
mark census: five further K562 marks (H3K4me2 ENCFF118MMT, H3K9ac
ENCFF866KTJ, H3K79me2 ENCFF003CLZ, H4K20me1 ENCFF143CUR, H2AFZ
ENCFF191EXE) and four further HeLa-S3 marks (H3K4me3 ENCFF699TXY,
H3K9me3 ENCFF761QZP, H3K27me3 ENCFF484EAZ, H3K36me3 ENCFF559FSM)
(\texttt{results/gap5\_encode\_sweep3.json}). All AUROCs sit between
$0.42$ and $0.55$ and all positive-side $|\rho| < 0.1$. Across
\emph{twenty-six} ENCODE tracks, four cell lines and every major
histone-modification class, chromatin state at $\pm 500$\,bp adds no
predictive signal for cell-free off-target cleavage. (Raw bigWigs are
re-fetched from ENCODE on demand per accession; see PROVENANCE.)
Chromosome-level context is also flat: on the hg38 slice (1{,}630
sites; Fig.~\ref{fig:karyotype}), per-chromosome mean cleavage ranges
$0.008\%$--$2.4\%$ with no significant chromosome effect (Kruskal--Wallis
$H=25.8$, $p=0.26$; \texttt{results/karyotype.json}). 685 Cameron
rows carry tiny negative cleavage entries (background-subtraction
artifacts, mean $-1.7\times10^{-4}$); we clip them to zero.

\section{Discovery: target-inherent transfer residual}
\label{sec:discovery}
Training on Doench FC+RES and scoring the DeepHF guides yields a per-guide
transfer residual (predicted rank minus measured rank). Across the three
independent nuclease screens of the same ${\sim}54$k guides, the residuals
replicate at $\rho=0.856$ (WT vs eSpCas9), $0.900$ (eSpCas9 vs SpCas9-HF1)
and $0.845$ (WT vs HF1), while the measured efficacies themselves correlate
at only ${\sim}0.73$: \textbf{the model's transfer error is more stable
across nucleases than the biological signal.} The residual is therefore a
target-inherent property (sequence and/or locus context), not a nuclease or
model artifact. A feature decomposition finds no single dominant driver
(max $|\rho|=0.12$ over the 11 DeepHF biofeatures; sequence GC
$\rho=-0.04$ after the encoding fix), i.e.\ a
distributed sequence-composition signature. Folding all guides with
ViennaRNA adds a twelfth candidate driver and it too fails
(post-encoding-fix full WT screen, 55{,}604 guides: minimum free energy
correlates with the residual at only $\rho=-0.030$; pre-fix it was
$-0.085$, and appending MFE to the portability model moved cross-screen
prediction by $+0.0002$). Falsification was built in: the
third screen (HF1) was held out of the initial analysis and confirmed the
concordance. Two consequences: (i)~cross-assay portability is a measurable,
predictable property of a guide and can be reported as a design score --
no current tool exposes it; (ii)~transfer-failure analyses that blame
``model overfitting'' are incomplete: a large, stable, target-inherent
component sits in the assay shift itself. A companion attempt to turn
crisprSQL's non-NGG pairs into an empirical PAM-activity atlas that beats
CFD failed its out-of-sample test (mean guide-held-out $\rho$: atlas
$0.464$ vs CFD $0.469$) and is preserved as a negative
(\texttt{results/pam\_atlas\_falsification.json}).

\subsection{Portability correction: rescuing cross-dataset transfer}
\label{sec:porta}
If the residual is target-inherent and predictable, it should be
\emph{correctable}. We test this directly. A ridge \emph{portability model}
is fit on the WT-SpCas9 screen only: inputs are the guide's sequence
features and DeepHF biofeatures, and the target is the per-guide transfer
residual (rank of the Doench-FC+RES prediction minus rank of the measured
WT activity). The model never sees any label from the destination screen.
Applied zero-shot to SpCas9-HF1, it predicts the per-guide residual at
$\rho=0.763$ on all 56{,}888 guides and $\rho=0.677$ on the 2{,}609 guides
whose sequences never occur in the WT training screen. Subtracting the
predicted residual from the raw Doench transfer ranking lifts transfer
performance from $\rho=0.080$ to $\rho=0.524$; on
strictly unseen guides it goes from $0.034$ to $0.352$.
On eSpCas9 the same WT-trained model lifts transfer from $0.052$ to
$0.562$ (n$=$58{,}617). Corrected performance approaches the within-screen
CNN baseline ($0.55$--$0.61$), i.e.\ the correction recovers most of the
signal that domain shift destroys. This is a new capability: Rule Set 3,
CRISPRon, and DeepHF-class models are all single-domain; none reports or
exploits a per-guide portability score. We expose it as the
\texttt{crisprgap} portability tool: given a guide set and one calibration
screen, it outputs a per-guide portability score and a corrected ranking.

\section{Limitations}
crisprSQL aggregates 17 studies with different assays and sensitivities;
its 94\% positive prevalence and 1,494 validated negatives cap
discrimination and make cross-study confounds unavoidable (we split by
guide\,$\times$\,study to bound leakage). Our off-target AUROCs
($0.51$--$0.58$) are honest for this split but below what single-study
evaluations report; we claim superiority only over MIT/CFD measured on the
identical split. The pegRNA transfer negative is established for
sequence-motif transfer from SpCas9 efficacy; parameter transfer from
larger prime-editing-specific pretraining remains open. Epigenetic tracks
in crisprSQL are study-confounded. Models are intentionally small
(sandbox-scale); scaling laws are out of scope.

\section{Tool documentation}
Each gap ships as a callable tool: \texttt{crisprgap.crossdata} (gap 1
harness), \texttt{crisprgap.calibration} + \texttt{crisprgap.cfd} (gap 2
calibrated scorer with MIT/CFD baselines), \texttt{crisprgap.train\_pegrna}
+ \texttt{crisprgap.models.transfer\_cnn} (gap 3 predictor),
\texttt{crisprgap.models.offtarget\_v2} (gaps 4--5 PAM/chromatin-aware
scorer). All tools are covered by the hermetic pytest suite.

\subsection{PORTA: portability scoring and corrected transfer}
\label{sec:porta-tool}
PORTA packages the discovery as a design tool. Inputs: a source-trained
efficacy model $f$, one \emph{calibration screen} measuring a guide library
(any public screen whose guides overlap the target library), and a set of
query guides with sequences and optional biofeatures. Outputs per guide:
(i)~the \emph{portability score} $\hat r(g) = m(\phi(g), b(g))$ -- the
predicted normalized-rank residual of $f$ on the calibration screen --
and (ii)~the corrected ranking $\tilde p(g) = \mathrm{nrank}(f(g)) -
\hat r(g)$. Interpretation: guides in the top PORTA tail are ones the
source model systematically over-ranks \emph{for assay reasons}, so their
low transfer rank should not disqualify them; the bottom tail flags
guides likely to disappoint on a new assay despite high source scores.
Reference usage:
\begin{verbatim}
from crisprgap.porta import fit_portability, correct
m = fit_portability(model, calib_screen)      # ridge on rank residuals
score = m.score(seqs, biofeatures)            # per-guide portability
ranking = correct(model, seqs, biofeatures)   # corrected ranks
\end{verbatim}
Validation for this exact configuration (Doench source, DeepHF WT
calibration, HF1/eSpCas9 destination) is Table~9; the correction is
exact when residual replication and portability prediction are perfect
(Proposition~\ref{prop:porta}) and degrades gracefully with their
product.
